 Water Ion Limits for Brewing

## Key Ions

### Calcium ($Ca^{2+}$)

* **Recommended water levels**: 30–150 ppm.
* **Details**: Calcium is probably the most important cation to be found in brewing water. Calcium is beneficial in most steps of the brewing phase, from its positive effect on alpha amylase enzymes in the mash to the boil, fermentation, and yeast flocculation. If you are starting with RO, distilled, or very soft water, you should look to boost your calcium levels with one of several calcium-containing salts and bases such as calcium chloride ($CaCl_{2}$), gypsum ($CaSO_{4}$), chalk ($CaCO_{3}$), or lime ($CaOH$).

### Magnesium ($Mg^{2+}$)

* **Recommended water levels**: 5–30 ppm.
* **Details**: Magnesium's benefits are similar in nature to those of calcium, aiding in several key steps during the mash. One important detail is that magnesium will see a large boost in the mash as it occurs naturally in malt. It can also affect the flavor profile of the beer, adding bitterness at lower levels and astringency at higher levels. It is recommended that you do not exceed 50 ppm Mg in your brewing water, and excessive amounts are cited as causing a laxative effect. Epsom salts ($MgSO_{4}$) are the most common way for brewers to boost their Mg levels.

### Sodium ($Na$)

* **Recommended water levels**: 0–50 ppm.
* **Details**: Sodium is not known to have any beneficial effects on the mash or fermentation, but it affects the mouthfeel of the beer. This means you can add sodium at any point during production, not just on brew day. While 50 ppm is recommended as the max level, certain beer styles may benefit from higher levels of sodium from table salt (like Gose), and it can be a nice touch in darker beers like stouts. Table salt ($NaCl$) and baking soda ($Na_{2}CO_{3}$) are the two most common ways for homebrewers to boost sodium.

### Bicarbonate ($HCO_{3}^{-}$) / Carbonate ($CO_{3}^{2-}$)

* **Recommended water levels**: Varies (0–50 ppm for pale beers; 50–150 ppm for amber to brown color beers; 150–300 ppm for dark to jet-black beers).
* **Details**: This is the ion that brewers tend to focus on the most due to its extreme importance in determining the pH of the mash and beer, and for the most part can be considered an unwanted ion in all but darker colored beers. Carbonate will precipitate out in the presence of calcium, which is why it is called temporary hardness. Baking soda ($Na_{2}CO_{3}$) and chalk ($CaCO_{3}$) are commonly used to boost carbonate levels.

### Chloride ($Cl^{-}$)

* **Recommended water levels**: 0–300 ppm.
* **Details**: Chloride has been cited as a beneficial ion during the mash, but not required. It has traditionally been used to boost the mouthfeel and creaminess of malt-forward beers, but recently homebrewers have been using chloride in certain IPAs to enhance the character of the beer. Calcium chloride ($CaCl_{2}$) and table salt ($NaCl$) are commonly used to boost chloride levels.

### Sulfate ($SO_{4}^{2-}$)

* **Recommended water levels**: 20–150 ppm.
* **Details**: For beers where brewers look for a hop bite, levels go up to 350 ppm to "Burtonize" the water. Above 400 ppm, the effects of sulfate turn harsh and should be avoided. Note that certain water tests provide sulfate levels "as-S"; to convert to actual sulfate levels, multiply by 3. Gypsum ($CaSO_{4}$) and Epsom salts ($MgSO_{4}$) are two common salts used to boost sulfate levels.

## Other Ions

There are a few other ions found in water reports worth noting, though brewers would never add them[cite: 2]. In a worst-case scenario, brewing water would need to be supplemented with RO or distilled water to bring these levels down into an acceptable range[cite: 2].

* **Potassium ($K^{+}$)**: Should not exceed 10 ppm[cite: 2].
* **Iron ($Fe$)**: Should not exceed 0.1 ppm[cite: 2].
* **Manganese ($Mn$)**: Should not exceed 0.05 ppm[cite: 2].
* **Nitrate ($NO_{3}^{-}$)**: Should not exceed 44 ppm[cite: 2]. Sometimes provided "as-N"; if so, multiply the value by 4.4 to obtain actual ppm nitrates[cite: 2].

## Chlorine and Fluoride

### Chlorine

If you are on a municipal water system, your water is likely treated with some form of chlorine[cite: 2]. This is a serious problem for brewers as chlorine converts into chlorophenols, which provide a medicinal/mouthwash flavor even at very low levels (detectable starting at just 10 parts per billion, ppb)[cite: 2]. Fortunately, chlorine is easily treated by even beginner brewers[cite: 2].

### Fluoride

Municipal fluoridated water brings mixed news: an RO water system is required to remove its presence, but fluoride ultimately has little impact on the final beer[cite: 2].
